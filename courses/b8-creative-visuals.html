Part 1 · covers 2026 image-gen for marketing

On-brand image generation 6 min live

The difference between one lucky image and a consistent brand look is the prompt recipe. A strong image prompt is not a wish - it is a structure: subject, style, palette, mood, and aspect ratio, plus a brand style reference so every image belongs to the same family. Nail the recipe once and you can reproduce the look across an ad, a social post, and a banner.

LiveThe recipe: subject, style, palette, mood, aspect3 min▶

Vague prompts give you a slot machine - occasionally brilliant, never repeatable. A structured prompt gives you a recipe you can rerun. Say what is in frame, how it should look, the exact brand colours, the feeling, and the size, then anchor it with a reference so the family holds.

  • Be specific about each ingredient. "A laptop on a warm desk, clean flat-illustration style, deep-pink and cream palette, calm and focused mood, 16:9" beats "a nice picture of a note-taking app".
  • Use brand style references. Most tools let you feed a reference image or a saved style. That is how you stop every image looking like a different company made it.
  • Know the tool for the job. Midjourney for stylised concepts, GPT image / DALL-E for prompt-following and text-in-image, Nano Banana for edits and consistency, Canva for on-brand templated production. Match the tool to the task.
★ Try it now (any AI image tool) - a recipe, not a wishA single laptop on a warm wooden desk with a coffee cup, clean flat-vector illustration style, deep-pink (#C2185B) and cream palette, calm and focused mood, soft even lighting, 16:9 aspect ratio, generous negative space for a headline on the left.
Lock the recipe, then vary one thing Once a recipe gives you an on-brand image, keep the style, palette, and mood fixed and change only the subject for the next asset. That single discipline is what makes a set look like a set.
Part 2 · covers the IP, likeness & rights canon - the loud rail

The IP, likeness & rights minefield 6 min live

This is the rail that can end a campaign, so read it twice. AI images can raise real copyright, likeness, and trademark problems - and "the AI made it" is not a defence. Before any AI visual goes near a public channel, it gets a rights check and a human sign-off. No exceptions.

Read this first

An AI image is an input you are responsible for, not a finished clearance. The tool does not guarantee you own the output, that it is free of someone else's copyright, or that it is safe to use commercially. That responsibility sits with you and your brand. Treat every generated image as unverified until you have checked it.

LiveThe three risks: copyright, likeness, trademark3 min▶

Three separate landmines, each with its own guardrail. Learn to spot all three before you prompt, not after you publish.

RiskGuardrail
Copyright - output trained on or mimicking copyrighted work, or a named living artist's styleDo not prompt "in the style of [living artist]"; use generic style words; prefer rights-clear inputs and your own references
Likeness / right of publicity - real people, celebrities, deepfakes, recognisable facesNo real or identifiable people without written consent; never generate a public figure; no synthetic "customer" faces presented as real
Trademark - logos, brand marks, another company's product or packaging in frameKeep other brands' logos and trade dress out of the image; do not imply endorsement you do not have
Commercial-use terms - the tool's own licence for paid/business useRead the tool's terms; confirm your plan permits commercial use and check who owns the output
Disclosure - where AI-generated imagery must be labelledDisclose AI-generated visuals where the platform or law (FTC / ASA) requires; never pass a synthetic image off as a real photo of a real event
The non-negotiable Human review before anything publishes. Every AI visual gets a person checking copyright, likeness, trademark, the tool's commercial terms, and disclosure - and signing off - before it reaches a public channel. This is rail one and the IP rail together, and it is the loudest rule in the course.
Self-studyRights-clear inputs and the "would this hold up" test2 min read▶
  • Use rights-clear inputs. Your own photos, licensed stock, brand assets you own - not images scraped off the web fed in as references.
  • Avoid naming living artists. "in the style of [famous living artist]" invites a copyright and moral-rights problem. Describe the look in generic terms instead.
  • No real faces without consent. Do not generate identifiable real people, and do not create synthetic people you then present as genuine customers.
  • Check commercial-use terms per tool. Free tiers and business tiers differ on ownership and commercial rights. Read them for every tool you use.
  • Disclose where required. Follow platform and FTC / ASA rules on labelling AI-generated content; never deceive.
Part 3 · scaling the look without losing the brand

Brand consistency at volume 4 min live

One on-brand image is a demo; fifty on-brand images is a system. The way you scale without drifting is presets, reference sets, and human art direction - and a rule you carry from b2: the visual serves the message, it does not replace it.

LivePresets, do/don't refs, human art direction2 min▶

Consistency at volume comes from reuse and judgement, not from generating more and hoping. Save what works and steer what does not.

  • Save the recipe as a preset. Lock the style, palette, mood, and reference so anyone on the team produces the same family.
  • Keep do/don't reference boards. Show examples of on-brand and off-brand output so the tool - and the team - has a target.
  • Art-direct, do not just accept. The human picks, crops, adjusts, and rejects. AI proposes; you direct. That is where the brand actually lives.
LiveThe visual serves the message2 min▶

A striking image that says nothing about Cadence is decoration, not marketing. Tie every visual back to the positioning from b2 and the message it supports. Pretty is the floor; on-message is the point.

The two-question gate for any visual "Does this look like Cadence?" and "Does this carry the message?" If either answer is no, it does not ship - however good the image looks on its own. Craft in service of the message, always.
Demo 1 of 2

Craft an on-brand Cadence visual set ★ 13 min · everyone builds

Build a small, coherent visual set - one ad image and a couple of social images - from a single consistency recipe, so they read as one brand rather than three random pictures.

Write the recipe first, in a chat AI: subject options for Cadence, plus a fixed style, palette (deep pink and cream), mood, and the aspect ratios you need (16:9 ad, 1:1 and 4:5 social). Save it as your preset.

Generate the ad image in your image tool using the recipe. Then generate the social images by keeping style, palette, and mood fixed and changing only the subject and aspect ratio.

Use a brand style reference if the tool supports it - a saved style, a reference image you own, or a first accepted image used as the anchor for the rest.

Art-direct the set. Reject anything off-palette or off-mood and regenerate. Confirm all three look like they came from the same brand.

Check each against the message: does this ad or post actually support Cadence's positioning, or is it just a nice picture? Cut the decoration.

Prompt A - the slot-machine prompt (inconsistent + risky)Make me a cool marketing image for a note-taking app, in the style of a famous modern artist, with a happy customer in it.
★ Prompt B - a consistency recipe (reusable)Help me build an on-brand image recipe for Cadence (an AI note-taker), then give me 3 prompts that share it. Fixed brand recipe: clean flat-vector illustration style; deep-pink (#C2185B) and cream palette; calm, focused, uncluttered mood; soft even lighting; generous negative space. No real or identifiable people. No named living artist's style. No other brands' logos. Give me 3 ready-to-run image prompts using this exact recipe, varying only the subject: 1) a hero ad image, 16:9, space for a headline; 2) a square social image, 1:1; 3) a portrait social image, 4:5. Keep style, palette, and mood identical across all three so they read as one set.
Real world

The set that finally looked like a brand. A team kept generating one-off images that were each fine but never matched - a different vibe every post. Once they locked a single recipe and reused it, their feed suddenly read as one confident brand instead of a stock-image grab bag. Consistency, not cleverness, was what made it look professional.

Demo 2 of 2

Run the rights & brand checklist before "publish" ★ 8 min · build your own

The gate that keeps the brand safe. Take each image from Demo 1 and run it through an IP, likeness, and rights check plus a brand check, before anything is treated as ready to publish.

Build the checklist with a chat AI, tailored to your tools: one line each for copyright, likeness / real people, trademark / other logos, the tool's commercial-use terms, disclosure, and the two brand questions.

Run every image through it, honestly. Any "no" or "not sure" blocks the image until it is fixed or replaced. A "not sure" on rights is a no.

Regenerate anything that fails - drop a named artist reference, remove a stray logo, swap a too-realistic face for a clearly illustrated one - and re-run the check.

Record the human sign-off. Note who reviewed each image and that it passed. That record is what turns "we should be careful" into an actual control.

★ The rights + brand checklist promptBuild me a pre-publish checklist for AI-generated marketing images, as short yes/no questions. I use [name your image tools]. Cover: - Copyright: does it copy a specific work or mimic a named living artist's style? - Likeness: any real or identifiable person, or a synthetic face shown as a real customer? - Trademark: any other brand's logo, product, or packaging in frame? - Commercial use: does my plan on this tool allow commercial use, and who owns the output? - Disclosure: does this need an AI-generated label on our platform or under FTC/ASA? - Brand: does it look like Cadence, and does it carry the message (not just decoration)? End with: "Any NO or NOT SURE blocks publish until fixed. Record who signed off."
Why this is the loud rail Text mistakes you can edit; a published image that infringes copyright, uses someone's likeness, or misleads is a public, legal problem. The checklist plus a named human sign-off is cheap insurance against an expensive headline. Never let an AI visual skip it.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Finish both demos. If you only do one, do the checklist - the rights habit matters more than any single image.
  • Build a real on-brand visual set for a project of your own using one locked recipe, and confirm the images read as one family.
  • Run every image through your IP, likeness, and rights checklist, and note the human sign-off for each. Keep the record.
  • Read the commercial-use terms for each AI image tool you actually use. Write down, in one line, whether your plan allows commercial use and who owns the output.
  • Optional: find one image you were tempted to publish that fails the check on likeness or a stray logo. That near-miss is the lesson. Bring it to session b9.

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · How do you get a consistent, on-brand look across many AI images?

Consistency is a recipe, not luck. Fix the style, palette, mood, and a reference; vary only the subject. Reusing the recipe is what makes a set read as one brand.

2 · Which of these is a real legal risk with AI-generated marketing images?

All three are live risks, plus the tool's commercial-use terms and disclosure. "The AI made it" is not a defence - responsibility for the output sits with you and the brand.

3 · An AI ad image looks great. What has to happen before it publishes?

Human review before anything publishes is the loud rail. Check copyright, likeness, trademark, commercial terms, disclosure, and brand fit, and record the sign-off. A "not sure" on rights is a no.
